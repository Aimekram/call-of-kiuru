How it works

Every page shows a modelled spring bloom and compares it with that city's {{ baseline }} average. Spring bloom means full spring in the phenological calendar of Germany's weather service, DWD: early spring starts with hazel bloom, first spring with forsythia, full spring with apple bloom. Lilac first blooms about a week after the apples, well inside full spring. The model is the first-bloom index of the Extended Spring Indices (SI-x), published by Schwartz, Ault and Betancourt (2013) and used unchanged. No AI produces the dates: SI-x is a fixed set of published equations on daily temperatures, and the site's code is tested to give exactly the same days as USA-NPN's own. This page explains why it is modelled, why only Germany and Sweden, and how the average is calculated. How well the model matches observed bloom has its own page.

Why a model, not observations

Observed bloom is the ground truth: it is what the model is checked against. The model adds what observations alone can't give:

  • Every city, every spring since 1940. Sweden's open lilac records start in 2008, and Germany's are too sparse before 1951. The chart and the {{ baseline }} average need a date for every year.
  • One method throughout. Observer networks change: Sweden had about 20 lilac records nationwide in 2008, against 76 or more a year from 2010. The model calculates every year with the same published rule, independent of who was watching which bush.
  • A number within days. The temperatures it needs arrive about five days late. Germany's observers send in their dates once a year: by 18 October 2026, five months after this spring's bloom, 283 had reported, against about 910 for each of 2024 and 2025.

These counts are based on data from Deutscher Wetterdienst (CC BY 4.0) and from SLU Naturens Kalender (CC0).

Why only Germany and Sweden

A city is added only after the model has been checked against observed bloom there. Of about 20 European countries checked, only Germany and Sweden publish their lilac observations openly. PEP725, the pan-European phenology database, may hold more, under licences set per dataset since May 2026. Its lilac data has not been requested yet.

The calculation

  1. For each of the {{ baseline_length }} years in {{ baseline }}, the model gives the city a spring-bloom day from that year's temperatures. Days are counted from 1 January, which is day 1.
  2. The average is the plain mean of those {{ baseline_length }} days.
  3. A year's "days from average" is its own day minus that average. A minus sign means earlier, a plus sign later.

The average is of the model's own results, not of observed bloom. Both sides of the comparison come from the same model and the same temperature data, so an error that is the same every year cancels out.

Why the date says "around"

The average is rarely a whole day, so pages show the nearest whole day, as a date in a 365-day year. In a leap year the same day of the year falls one calendar date earlier.

Why {{ baseline }}

  • It is the World Meteorological Organization's current climatological standard normal: the most recent 30 years ending in a year that ends in 0.
  • The USA National Phenology Network compares against the same years in its maps of spring in the United States. Its averages come from different temperature data.

It is a recent period, and spring already came earlier in it than before. In the model, the {{ earlier }} average falls {{ earlier_gap_least }} to {{ earlier_gap_most }} days later than the {{ baseline }} one in these cities. "The same as average" means the same as a recent spring, not an earlier one.

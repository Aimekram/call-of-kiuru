How it works

Every page shows a modelled start of spring and compares it with that city's {{ baseline }} average. This page explains why it is modelled, how well the model matches observed bloom, and how the average is calculated.

Why a model, not observations

Observed bloom is the ground truth: it is what the model is checked against, below. The model adds what observations alone can't give:

  • Every city, every spring since 1940. Sweden's open lilac records start in 2008, and Germany's are too sparse before 1951. The chart and the {{ baseline }} average need a date for every year.
  • One method throughout. Observer networks change: Sweden had about 20 lilac records nationwide in 2008, against 76 or more a year from 2010. The model calculates every year with the same published rule, independent of who was watching which bush.
  • A number within days. The temperatures it needs arrive about five days late. Germany's observers send in their dates once a year: by 18 October 2026, five months after this spring's bloom, 283 had reported, against about 910 for each of 2024 and 2025.

The ranges come from resampling whole springs. Cities share each year's weather, so the evidence is the number of springs, not of city-years.

The model's dates run early: by 3.7 days on average in Germany and 0.3 days in Sweden. It averages a lilac and two honeysuckles, and the honeysuckles bloom earlier, while the observations are lilac only. Its lilac part alone is 0.2 days off in Germany. The offset does not change the numbers on this site, because each city is compared with the model's own average for that city.

The observations are based on data from Deutscher Wetterdienst (CC BY 4.0) and from SLU Naturens Kalender (CC0).

Limitations

  • Sweden's evidence is thinner: 17 springs, with a median of 3 to 4 observations per city and year, in some years one. The first two years, 2008 and 2009, pull it down. From 2010 on, the correlation is 0.82 (0.67–0.92).
  • Years before 1951 in Germany and before 2008 in Sweden are modelled only. There are too few observations to check them.
  • Against single lilac bushes rather than local medians, a European study found the model about a week off on average (Izquierdo-Verdiguier and Zurita-Milla, 2024).

Why only Germany and Sweden

A city is added only after the model has been checked against observed bloom there. Of about 20 European countries we looked at, only Germany and Sweden publish their lilac observations openly. PEP725, the pan-European phenology database, may hold more, under licences set per dataset since May 2026. We have not asked for its lilac data yet.

The calculation

  1. For each of the {{ baseline_length }} years in {{ baseline }}, the model gives the city a start-of-spring day from that year's temperatures. Days are counted from 1 January, which is day 1.
  2. The average is the plain mean of those {{ baseline_length }} days.
  3. A year's "days from average" is its own day minus that average. A minus sign means earlier, a plus sign later.

The average is of the model's own results, not of observed bloom. Both sides of the comparison come from the same model and the same temperature data, so an error that is the same every year cancels out.

Why the date says "around"

The average is rarely a whole day, so pages show the nearest whole day, as a date in a 365-day year. In a leap year the same day of the year falls one calendar date earlier.

Why {{ baseline }}

  • It is the World Meteorological Organization's current climatological standard normal: the most recent 30 years ending in a year that ends in 0.
  • The USA National Phenology Network compares against the same years in its maps of spring in the United States. Its averages come from different temperature data.

It is a recent period, and spring already came earlier in it than before. In the model, the {{ earlier }} average falls {{ earlier_gap_least }} to {{ earlier_gap_most }} days later than the {{ baseline }} one in these cities. "The same as average" means the same as a recent spring, not an earlier one.
